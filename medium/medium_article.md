# What If Time Isn't a Dimension at All?

## Einstein said time is the fourth dimension, with a famous minus sign attached. A new paper rebuilds his relativity with no time dimension, no minus sign, and every prediction intact.

![A clock dissolving into streaks of motion above a flat grid](medium/images/hero-time-as-motion.png)
*Time, in this picture, isn't somewhere you are. It's a measure of how far things move.*

---

For more than a century, physics has told us a strange story: time is a dimension. Space and time are woven into one fabric, spacetime, and your "now" is not my "now." Two people moving past each other can't even agree which events happen at the same moment.

It's one of the most successful ideas in science. GPS satellites correct for it every day. Particle accelerators depend on it. But it leaves a question almost everyone has asked at some point:

**If time is a dimension, why can't we walk around in it the way we walk around in space?**

My new paper takes that question seriously and proposes an answer that sounds almost too simple: **time isn't a dimension. It's a ratio of distances.**

📄 **Read the paper:** [*Relativity without a Time Dimension*](https://zenodo.org/records/24561378) (open access)

---

## The minus sign at the heart of physics

Every student of relativity meets a strange equation. In ordinary geometry, Pythagoras tells you how distances combine: square the sides, *add* them, and you get the square of the long side. Einstein's spacetime does the same thing, except time comes in with a *minus* sign. That one sign is what makes time "different," and it's the source of almost everything counter-intuitive about relativity.

Here's the surprise: **you don't need the minus sign.** Rearrange the same relation, and it becomes an ordinary right triangle:

![A right triangle: the long side is absolute time, the two short sides are your clock's time and the distance you travelled](medium/images/diagram-pythagoras-of-time.png)
*Pythagoras, applied to time. The long side is the same for everyone.*

- The **long side** is absolute time, the same for every observer in the universe.
- One **short side** is the distance you travelled.
- The other **short side** is what your own clock reads.

When you sit still, all of absolute time goes into your clock. When you move, some of it has to go into distance instead, so your clock reads less. That's time dilation, the effect measured on fast-moving particles and satellite clocks, and here it's nothing more than a triangle. The angle of the triangle tells you everything: its sine is your speed as a fraction of light speed, and its cosine is how fast your clock runs.

In the paper, the triangle sits in a four-dimensional space made only of *spatial* directions: the three we know, plus a hidden fourth one, called **y**. Moving through ordinary space tilts your path into y. Every relationship in the theory, in special relativity and in gravity alike, is a sum of squares. Mathematicians would say the geometry has signature (+,+,+,+): no minus signs anywhere.

The consequence is what makes the idea worth a paper. **Every observer can agree on a single, absolute "now."** All the measured effects of relativity survive: slowed clocks, shortened lengths, the speed of light being the same for everyone. What disappears is the idea that "now" is fundamentally relative. Light stops being the scaffolding of time and becomes what it looks like: a signal spreading out in a sphere, through which we read our clocks.

## Gravity: space flows, and the ground rises to meet you

Now for gravity, where the picture becomes vivid.

In this theory, **space flows outward from every mass**, the way the universe's expansion flows outward on cosmic scales. At the surface of the Earth, space streams outward at 11.2 km/s, which is exactly the escape velocity. Farther away the flow slows down.

![Plot: the flow speed of space falling from 11.2 km/s at the Earth's surface, and the tiny slowing of clocks held in place](medium/images/plot-outward-flow-earth.png)
*The outward flow of space around the Earth, and how much a clock held in place slows down: less than one part in a billion.*

Now think about what that means for you, standing on the ground. You aren't moving with the flowing space; you're holding still against it. In relativity, anything moving through space at some speed has its lengths stretched along the direction of travel and its clocks slowed. So you, the ground under your feet, and every atom of the Earth are **co-expanded along the direction of the flow**, and every clock on the Earth runs a little slow. It's the same rule that applies to a fast-moving spaceship, applied to space flowing past a planet.

That leads to a startling way of seeing something we experience every second.

When you drop a ball, we say gravity pulls it down. In this picture, the ball, once you let go, is no longer held against the flow. It stops co-expanding with the matter around it, and **the co-expanding ground rises up to meet it.** Falling is what it looks like when you're no longer carried along with everything else.

![Two panels: on the left, an apple pulled down to the ground; on the right, the apple holds still and the co-expanding ground rises to meet it](medium/images/illustration-falling-vs-rising.png)
*Same observation, two stories.*

This explains something Einstein had to take as a starting assumption: **why everything falls the same way**, feathers and bowling balls alike in a vacuum. Here it's no coincidence. All matter co-expands by exactly the same amount, so anything you release stops co-expanding in exactly the same way.

The effect is tiny at human scales. The Earth's radius, measured along the flow, is longer than its radius measured around its circumference by only about **1.5 millimetres**. All of gravity's pull at the surface comes with that sliver of co-expansion.

### The shape of the hidden direction

Where does the hidden direction y come in? The space around a mass is literally **tilted into it**, and the tilt is the flow: the faster space flows, the steeper the tilt. Plot the tilt around a star and you get a shape that has been sitting in the physics literature since 1916, when the Austrian physicist Ludwig Flamm drew it to visualize Einstein's solution for a star. In this theory, Flamm's funnel isn't just a picture. It's the actual shape of space in the hidden direction.

![A 3D funnel: the space around a mass, tilted into the hidden direction y, above a flat grid](medium/images/plot-inclined-space.png)
*The space around a mass, tilted into the hidden direction y. How far it tilts is also how far apart two clocks' settings are.*

## Does it work? Every test, passed

A theory of gravity has to clear a very high bar. Here, the mathematics delivered a pleasant surprise: the rule that sets how fast space flows turns out to be one of Einstein's own equations, written for the flowing space. Around a star, the geometry is *exactly* the one Einstein's theory predicts, not just approximately. So every test that general relativity passes, this picture passes too:

## The test that changed the theory

Science rarely moves in a straight line, and this paper didn't either.

An earlier version stretched lengths around a mass in **every** direction, not just along the flow. It looked elegant, and it passed all the classic tests. But when we worked out what it predicted for **neutron stars**, the densest objects that aren't black holes, it said a neutron star should fall about **4% more slowly** than a lighter star beside it.

Nature has run that experiment for us. In the constellation Taurus, a pulsar orbits together with two white dwarf stars, and astronomers timing its pulses have shown that the neutron star and the white dwarf fall the same way to within **two parts in a million**. That's twenty thousand times too precise for the old version.

![Bar chart: co-expansion in every direction predicts a neutron star falls 4% differently, far above the narrow band the triple-star pulsar allows; co-expansion along the flow predicts exactly zero](medium/images/plot-why-direction-matters.png)
*One pulsar, two stars, and a ruled-out idea.*

The fix came from relativity itself. In Einstein's theory, a moving ruler only changes length **along** its direction of motion, never sideways. Apply that same rule to matter in the flow of space, stretched along the flow and unchanged across it, and the problem disappears completely. The prediction for the pulsar becomes exactly zero, the same as Einstein's.

I think that's the most honest thing a theory can show you: a place where it could have failed, did fail, and was corrected by a principle it already contained.

## So what's new, if the predictions match Einstein?

A fair question. This construction makes the same predictions as general relativity, so no experiment can tell them apart. What it changes is **what the predictions mean**:

- **Time is not a dimension.** It's a ratio of distances, read off a right triangle.
- **There's no minus sign.** Every relation in the theory is ordinary Pythagorean geometry.
- **"Now" is absolute.** Every observer can recover the same universal time.
- **Falling has a mechanism.** The ground rises to meet you because you've stopped co-expanding with it.
- **The equivalence principle has a reason.** Everything falls alike because everything co-expands alike.

Physics has seen this kind of step before. Newton's mechanics was rewritten by Lagrange and Hamilton with the same predictions, and their versions later became the language of quantum mechanics. A new way of describing the same world can open doors the old one kept shut.

## What's next

The open questions are big ones:

- **Whose "now"?** Around a single star, absolute time is clear. How the "now" of the Earth joins up with the "now" of the Moon is still an open question.
- **A life of its own for y.** Right now the hidden direction is the tilt of space. Giving it dynamics of its own is the long-term goal: physics with no time dimension at all, from the ground up.
- **The expanding universe.** The same outward flow describes cosmic expansion. Working out the cosmology in this language is next.
