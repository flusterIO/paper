The idea is simple. When something moves, we give it a displacement along one extra spatial direction, called y. The slope of y is beta times gamma. Put that slope on a right triangle, and the Lorentz factor is just the length of the hypotenuse. That is section one. The first figure shows the triangles for three speeds, and the second shows that gamma is fixed by the slope alone. Time is not a dimension here. It is a ratio of distances, carried as a length.

Section two is the heart of the paper: classical simultaneity. Every event gets an absolute position and an absolute time, tau. A moving observer's ordinary clocks read gamma times c tau, minus that observer's y offset. Those are exactly the Lorentz coordinates. In the first panel, the tilted dashed lines are the moving observer's "simultaneous" lines. They are clock settings, not time. The green arrows are the y offsets. In the middle panel, along one absolute instant, each observer's clock offset is exactly minus y. In the last panel, every observer adds back their y and recovers the same instant.

The scatter plot makes the same point with four hundred random events. On the left, four observers disagree. On the right, once each corrects by their own y, all four agree on one absolute time.

Next, what every observer agrees on. Each observer measures a different length, the hypotenuse. The base, the absolute separation in ordinary space, never changes. That is also why the extra dimension can have a positive sign in the metric. The stacked plot then shows time dilation from the geometry: a moving clock loses exactly its own advance along y, so it ticks at one over gamma.

Section three is the synchronization gauge. Bounce light off a mirror against absolute time. The outgoing light rides with the outward flow of space and the returning light works against it, so the two legs take different times. Einstein's convention puts the reflection at the midpoint. The difference between that convention and the absolute reflection time is the y offset.

Section four turns to gravity. Around a mass, the same slope gives the Flamm paraboloid, the funnel in the 3D figure. It sits above a flat slice, the absolute slice of Painlevé–Gullstrand time. In this geometry space flows outward through that slice, in the same direction as the Hubble flow. Someone standing on Earth stays at rest while space streams out past them at the escape speed, about eleven kilometres per second. A static observer's measured length is the arc length up the funnel. Their clock rate is one over gamma. Then there is a check I like: the synchronization gap, computed from the time coordinates, and the height of the funnel, computed from the geometry. The two are calculated independently, and the curves lie on top of each other.

Section five puts one relation on a single plot, from Earth's surface to a neutron star, the Galaxy, and the Hubble flow. At the Hubble radius the flow reaches the speed of light. That is the Sciama identity.

Section six goes inside stars. A uniform star is a cap that joins the funnel smoothly. Its central pressure blows up at nine eighths of the Schwarzschild radius, the Buchdahl bound. The Tolman seven profile shows the same behaviour.

Section seven is the honest constraint. If the size of the extra dimension were free to vary, light would bend by only half the observed amount. The Cassini spacecraft rules that out. General relativity is recovered exactly when the stress along y takes one specific value, minus half the energy density for ordinary matter.

Section eight covers rotation. Frame dragging gives about forty-one milliarcseconds per year, against Gravity Probe B's thirty-seven, plus or minus seven. A rotating frame's synchronization gap is the Sagnac effect, about two hundred seven nanoseconds around the equator.

Section nine covers orbits. They come straight from the embedding: the innermost stable orbit at three Schwarzschild radii, and the perihelion advance of Mercury, about forty-three arcseconds per century.

Section ten is the one hypothesis that goes beyond general relativity. It proposes a characteristic acceleration tied to the expansion rate of the universe. The Milky Way curve shows that it has the right shape, and the candidate value is within about a factor of two of the fit. One galaxy cannot settle it. A sample of galaxies is the real test.

Section eleven makes that test with the SPARC sample: a hundred and fifty-three galaxies with measured rotation curves and baryonic mass models. Fitted together, the scale comes out at 0.158 times the speed of light times the Hubble constant, right next to the candidate, one over two pi. The catch is in the second figure. Fitted one at a time, individual galaxies scatter around that value by more than their error bars allow. Whether that scatter is real is an argument the field is still having, so the paper reports it rather than claiming a win.

Section twelve asks where the absolute frame could show up. If clocks and lengths change with the Lorentz factor of motion through the cosmic frame, their exponents can be compared with experiment. This model's exponents, minus one, plus one and zero, are exactly special relativity, and clock and cavity experiments pin them down to better than one part in ten million. The direct test would be a yearly wobble between clocks and cavities as Earth's orbit changes its speed through the cosmic frame. So far there is none.

Finally, section thirteen audits the units and runs every self-check in the package. All of them pass. The last section lists what is established, what is assumed, and what is still open. Most importantly, the absolute frame has to be physically distinguished, and the paper identifies it with the cosmic flow.
